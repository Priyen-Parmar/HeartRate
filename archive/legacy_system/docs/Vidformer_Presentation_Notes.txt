SLIDE 1 — INPUT REPRESENTATION: HOW WE CONVERT VIDEO TO MODEL INPUT
======================================================================

What is rPPG and why video-based heart rate estimation works:

Every time the heart beats, it pumps blood through the body. This blood flows through tiny capillaries just beneath the skin on the face. As blood fills these capillaries, it causes extremely subtle color changes on the skin surface. These changes are completely invisible to the human eye, but a regular camera can pick them up. Remote Photoplethysmography (rPPG) is a technique that captures these micro-color fluctuations from video and reconstructs the pulse signal, which can then be used to calculate heart rate.

The key insight is that hemoglobin in blood absorbs green light more than red or blue. So when a pulse of blood arrives at the face, the green channel intensity drops slightly, and when blood recedes, it rises. This creates a periodic oscillation in the green channel that matches the heart rate.


How we prepare the input for the neural network:

Step 1 — Face video frames are first resized to 64 by 64 pixels. This is a small spatial resolution, but it is sufficient because the pulse signal is a global phenomenon across the skin. We do not need fine spatial detail. Smaller images also mean faster training and less overfitting.

Step 2 — We compute difference frames. For every frame at time t, we subtract the previous frame at time t-1. This gives us a new frame that shows only what changed between two consecutive time steps. The static parts of the face (shape, eye color, hair) all cancel out, and what remains is the tiny temporal variations, including the pulse signal. This is the most important preprocessing step because it amplifies exactly the signal we care about.

Step 3 — We stack the original RGB frames (3 channels) with the difference frames (3 channels) to create a 6-channel input. The original frames provide spatial context — they tell the model where the skin is, where the forehead is, where the cheeks are. The difference frames provide the temporal pulse signal — they tell the model how the color is changing over time. Together, these 6 channels give the model complete information.

The final input tensor has the shape [Batch, 6 channels, T frames, 64 height, 64 width]. Each input clip is 300 frames long, which corresponds to approximately 10 seconds of video at 30 frames per second. During these 10 seconds, the heart beats roughly 10 to 25 times (depending on heart rate), which gives the model enough periodic cycles to reliably identify the heart rate frequency.


Why 6 channels instead of just 3:

If we only use RGB frames, the model has to simultaneously learn two things — where the skin is AND how it changes over time. This is hard. By providing difference frames explicitly, we make the model's job easier. The difference frames are essentially a pre-computed temporal gradient. Research has shown that this simple trick improves heart rate estimation accuracy by 1-2 BPM compared to using raw RGB alone.


Data Augmentation during training:

During training, we apply gentle augmentations to increase data diversity and prevent overfitting. Random horizontal flip is applied because the face is roughly symmetric around the vertical axis. Mild Gaussian noise with standard deviation 0.01 is added to simulate real-world camera noise. Slight brightness jitter of plus or minus 10 percent is applied to simulate different lighting conditions. We also randomly crop 150 frames out of the 300 frame clip during training, which gives the model different temporal windows of the same recording. During validation and testing, we always use the full 300-frame clips for the most accurate BPM estimation.

We intentionally avoid aggressive augmentations like temporal masking (zeroing out segments of video) or strong color transformations because these destroy the very pulse signal we are trying to learn. The pulse signal is so subtle that heavy augmentation kills it.



SLIDE 2 — MODEL ARCHITECTURE: PhysFormerX v3
==============================================

Overview of the architecture:

The model takes the 6-channel video input and transforms it into a 1D PPG (photoplethysmography) waveform — essentially the pulse signal reconstructed from video. The architecture has three main parts: a 3D convolutional backbone for spatiotemporal feature extraction, a temporal convolution head for signal refinement, and an FFT-based BPM estimator. The total model has approximately 1.5 million trainable parameters.


Part 1 — The 3D ResNet Backbone:

The backbone is built from 3D convolutional layers, meaning each convolution kernel slides across three dimensions — height, width, and time simultaneously. This is fundamentally different from 2D convolutions used in image classification. 3D convolutions can capture spatiotemporal patterns, for example, the model can learn that the green intensity on the cheek decreases slightly at a regular rhythm, which is exactly the heart rate signal.

The backbone starts with a stem layer. This is a single 3D convolution that takes the 6-channel input and expands it to 32 feature channels. The convolution kernel is (1, 5, 5), meaning it does not mix time steps at this stage but applies a 5 by 5 spatial filter. This is followed by batch normalization, ReLU activation, and a spatial max pooling layer that reduces the spatial dimensions by half (64 to 32 pixels).

After the stem, there are four residual blocks. 

The first residual block (Layer 1) takes 32 channels to 64 channels with spatial stride (1, 2, 2). This means the spatial dimensions are halved again (32 to 16 pixels) but the temporal dimension is fully preserved. Each residual block contains two 3D convolution layers with batch normalization and ReLU activation, plus a skip connection that allows gradients to flow directly through the block. If the input and output dimensions differ, the skip connection uses a 1x1 convolution to match them.

The second residual block (Layer 2) keeps 64 channels and uses stride 1, so dimensions remain unchanged at [64, T, 16, 16]. This block provides additional feature refinement without downsampling.

The third residual block (Layer 3) goes from 64 to 128 channels with spatial stride (1, 2, 2), reducing spatial size to [128, T, 8, 8].

The fourth residual block (Layer 4) keeps 128 channels with stride 1, output is [128, T, 8, 8].

A critical design choice here is that the temporal dimension T is never reduced throughout the backbone. All spatial downsampling strides are (1, 2, 2) — the 1 in the first position means "do not downsample time." This is essential because our output must be a time-series with T values. If we lost temporal resolution, we could not reconstruct a proper PPG waveform.

After Layer 4, we apply Adaptive Average Pooling with output size (None, 1, 1). This collapses the remaining 8 by 8 spatial dimensions into a single point per time step, giving us a feature vector of shape [128, T]. Each of these 128-dimensional vectors summarizes the entire spatial information of the face at one particular time step.


Squeeze-and-Excitation (SE) Attention inside each ResBlock:

Every residual block includes a Squeeze-and-Excitation module. This is a lightweight channel attention mechanism that works as follows. First, the "squeeze" step applies global average pooling to produce a single number per channel — this captures the global average activation of each feature channel. Then, the "excitation" step passes these numbers through two small fully connected layers (with a bottleneck reduction factor of 8) followed by a sigmoid activation. The output is a set of weights between 0 and 1, one per channel. These weights are multiplied back with the original features.

What this does is let the model learn which feature channels are most useful for pulse detection and amplify them, while suppressing noisy or irrelevant channels. For example, features related to green channel intensity changes will likely get high weights, while features related to static texture patterns will get low weights. This is a form of learned attention that significantly improves the model's ability to focus on the pulse-relevant information.


Part 2 — The Temporal Convolution Head:

After the backbone produces a 128-dimensional feature vector per time step, the temporal head processes this sequence to produce the final PPG waveform.

The first layer is a 1D convolution with 128 input channels, 64 output channels, and kernel size 5, followed by batch normalization and ReLU. The kernel size 5 means each output is computed from 5 consecutive time steps (2 before, the current, 2 after). This provides local temporal smoothing.

The second layer is another 1D convolution with 64 input and 64 output channels, also with kernel size 5. This provides a second round of temporal smoothing and feature refinement. The effective receptive field after two layers of kernel 5 is 9 time steps, giving the head enough temporal context.

After these two layers, we apply dropout of 0.1 (randomly zero out 10 percent of features during training) to prevent the head from overfitting. Finally, a 1 by 1 convolution maps from 64 channels to 1 channel, producing the scalar PPG value at each time step.

The output is a 1D signal of length T — this is the predicted PPG waveform. It should look like a clean, periodic pulse signal if the model has learned correctly.


Part 3 — BPM Extraction via FFT:

To convert the predicted PPG waveform into a heart rate value, we use Fast Fourier Transform. The FFT converts the time-domain signal into the frequency domain, where each frequency gets a power value indicating how strongly that frequency is present in the signal.

We apply a Hann window to the signal before FFT to reduce spectral leakage (edge effects). Then we compute the Power Spectral Density and look only at the valid heart rate frequency range: 0.75 Hz to 2.5 Hz, which corresponds to 45 BPM to 150 BPM. The frequency with the highest power in this range is the dominant heart rate frequency. We multiply this frequency by 60 to convert from Hz (beats per second) to BPM (beats per minute).

This spectral approach is much more robust than trying to count individual peaks in the time-domain signal, because the PPG waveform can be noisy, and peak counting is error-prone.



SLIDE 3 — TRAINING STRATEGY, LOSS FUNCTION, AND EVALUATION
=============================================================

The Loss Function — Three components working together:

Training a neural network requires a loss function that tells the model how far its predictions are from the ground truth. We use a combination of three complementary loss functions, each capturing a different aspect of prediction quality.

The first and most important component is the Negative Pearson Correlation Loss. Pearson correlation measures how well the predicted PPG waveform's shape matches the true PPG waveform, on a scale from -1 (perfectly inverted) to +1 (perfect match). The loss is computed as (1 minus correlation), so a perfect prediction gives loss = 0 and a bad prediction gives loss close to 2. The key advantage of Pearson loss is that it does not care about the absolute amplitude or DC offset of the signals — it only measures shape similarity. This is important because the model and the ground truth sensor may produce signals at very different scales, but as long as the temporal pattern (the pulse rhythm) matches, the Pearson correlation will be high.

The second component is the Frequency Cross-Entropy Loss. This is a more direct approach to optimizing BPM accuracy. It works by computing the FFT of both the predicted and target signals, extracting only the frequency bins in the heart rate range (0.75 to 2.5 Hz), converting both spectra into probability distributions using softmax, and then computing the KL-divergence between them. The target spectrum is sharpened using a temperature parameter of 10 to create a peaked distribution around the true heart rate frequency. This loss forces the model to concentrate its spectral energy at the correct frequency, which directly translates to correct BPM estimation. Without this loss, the model might produce a waveform that looks similar to the target (low Pearson loss) but has its spectral peak at a slightly wrong frequency, leading to BPM error.

The third component is Smooth L1 Loss, which is a standard regression loss that penalizes the difference between predicted and true waveform values. It provides direct amplitude-level supervision and adds training stability. Smooth L1 is more robust to outliers than plain L1 or MSE loss.

The total loss is combined as: Total = 1.0 times Pearson Loss + 0.5 times Frequency CE Loss + 0.1 times Smooth L1 Loss. The Pearson loss gets the highest weight because waveform shape is the most important, followed by frequency alignment for BPM accuracy, and finally amplitude regression for stability.


Optimizer and Learning Rate Schedule:

We use AdamW optimizer, which is Adam with decoupled weight decay. Weight decay of 5e-5 provides mild L2 regularization to prevent overfitting. The learning rate follows a OneCycleLR schedule with three phases: a warmup phase covering the first 10 percent of training where the learning rate linearly increases from 3e-5 (one-tenth of the max) to the peak of 3e-4, followed by a cosine annealing phase where the learning rate gradually decreases, and finally converging to a very small value of 3e-7 by the end of training. This schedule allows the model to explore boldly at the beginning (high learning rate) and then fine-tune carefully near the end (low learning rate). The learning rate is stepped after every batch (not every epoch), which gives smoother annealing.

Gradient clipping at 2.0 is applied to prevent gradient explosions, which can occur when the frequency-domain loss produces large gradients.


Exponential Moving Average (EMA):

Instead of using the model's current weights for validation and testing, we maintain an exponential moving average of the weights with decay 0.998. At each training step, the EMA weights are updated as: EMA = 0.998 times old EMA + 0.002 times current weights. This produces a smoothed version of the model that averages out the noise from individual training steps. EMA typically gives 0.5 to 1 BPM better test MAE compared to using raw model weights.


Subject-Level Data Split:

The dataset is split into 70 percent training, 15 percent validation, and 15 percent test — but the split is done by subject ID, not by individual chunks. This means all chunks from one person go into the same split. If we split randomly by chunk, the model could see different chunks from the same person in both training and test sets, which would inflate the results because the model has already learned that person's skin characteristics. Subject-level splitting ensures we are testing on completely unseen people, which is the only meaningful evaluation for a real-world system.


Early Stopping:

We monitor the validation BPM MAE after every epoch. If it does not improve for 15 consecutive epochs (the patience parameter), training is stopped early. The model checkpoint with the best validation MAE is saved and used for final testing. This prevents the model from overfitting to the training data if trained for too many epochs.


Evaluation Metric:

The primary metric is Mean Absolute Error (MAE) in BPM. For every test clip, we compute the predicted BPM from the model's output waveform (using FFT peak detection) and compare it to the true BPM from the ground truth waveform. The absolute difference is computed, and then we average across all test clips.

MAE = (1/N) times the sum of absolute(predicted BPM minus true BPM)

Our target is a test MAE of less than 3 BPM, meaning on average the predicted heart rate is within 3 beats per minute of the true value. For context, a clinical pulse oximeter typically has an accuracy of plus or minus 2 BPM, so achieving under 3 BPM from a regular webcam video (no contact sensor) is considered very good performance.

Mixed precision training is used on GPU (float16 for forward pass, float32 for loss computation) to speed up training by approximately 2x without affecting accuracy. The frequency-domain loss computation is always done in float32 to avoid numerical issues with FFT.
